Every few months someone publishes a piece called something like "You probably don't need X" and it gets passed around developer circles with knowing nods. Then a week later, the same communities are debating whether to adopt the framework that replaces X. The meta-lesson never quite lands.

I want to make the case slowly, without polemic, for choosing boring technology — not as a permanent philosophy, but as a deliberate default.

## What "boring" actually means

**Boring doesn't mean simple.** Postgres is boring. It's also extraordinarily capable. React is becoming boring. It handles genuinely complex UI problems well. Boring means: the failure modes are known, the documentation is deep, the community has seen your bug before, and you can hire for it.

The opposite of boring isn't exciting — it's *risky*. A new framework might be better in important ways. It might also have an unfiled issue that corrupts your data under a specific race condition that you'll hit in production at 2am. Boring stacks have usually had their 2am moments already. The fixes are in the docs.

## The hidden cost of novelty

There's a tax on every new technology you adopt. You pay it in:

- **Time spent reading source code** because the documentation doesn't cover your use case yet
- **Upstream bugs** that block your work and require workarounds or forks
- **Onboarding friction** when new team members join
- **Migration costs** when the project is abandoned or pivots

This tax is real but invisible on the adoption side. The pitch deck for any new framework shows the 80% case — the clean example where everything works beautifully. It doesn't show the 20% where you're fighting the framework's opinions about things you don't care about.

## When to take the risk

None of this means never adopt new technology. There are cases where the novelty tax is worth paying:

- The problem genuinely can't be solved well with boring tools
- The team is small enough that onboarding cost is low
- The new tool has a path to becoming boring (strong team, clear roadmap, growing adoption)
- You're explicitly building expertise and the cost is budgeted

The key word is *deliberately*. Picking a new framework because it's interesting is different from picking it because it solves a specific problem you've identified. Both are valid choices, but only one of them will make sense to your future self when you're debugging it.

## The compounding value of depth

Here's the underrated part: boring stacks compound. Every year you spend with Postgres, you get better at Postgres. You learn the query planner quirks, the indexing tradeoffs, the backup patterns. That knowledge doesn't evaporate when a new database appears. It deepens your ability to evaluate the new database on its actual merits.

Novelty, by contrast, keeps resetting the clock. You never get deep. You get a thin layer of familiarity with a long tail of technologies, which is useful for conversation but not for shipping reliable software under pressure.

## A practical heuristic

Before adopting something new, I ask: **could I solve this problem in two days with boring tools, even if the solution is less elegant?** If yes, I usually choose boring. The elegance of the new approach isn't worth the cost unless I'm also buying something else — performance, a capability gap filled, a team skill built deliberately.

This isn't conservatism for its own sake. It's a bias toward shipping and maintaining, which is most of what software actually is.
