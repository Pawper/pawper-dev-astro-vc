Every portfolio I've built before this one felt like a document. A formatted list of things I've done, with appropriate visual weight applied to the things I want you to notice first. Functional, credentialed, forgettable.

The problem isn't effort — I'd put effort into all of them. The problem is that a document asks you to read it. A place asks you to explore it. Those are different experiences, and they create different impressions.

## The question I kept asking

At some point during the early design work I started asking a single question about every decision: does this feel like a place, or does it feel like a page?

It sounds vague, but it turned out to be surprisingly generative. A place has depth. A place has texture. A place rewards the person who slows down. A page just needs to be skimmed.

What changed when I asked it: I stopped optimizing for information density and started optimizing for atmosphere. Not at the expense of content — the content is still there, still retrievable — but the first impression stopped being "here are my credentials" and started being "here is a world I made."

## Building the backdrop

The literal backdrop — a blurred alpine photograph behind frosted glass panels — was one of the earlier decisions and one I second-guessed the most. It's a strong visual choice. It can read as showy.

But the more I worked with it, the more I understood why it worked. The mountain is always there. You're aware of it even when you're reading text. It creates a sense of context — that the panels are floating in front of something real, not sitting on an empty void.

The alternative, a dark gradient or flat color, would have been safer. It also would have been a surface rather than a space. The mountain turns the UI into a foreground, which changes the feeling of everything sitting in front of it.

## The LCARS reference

The control panel aesthetic — the colored arcs, the monospaced labels, the CASE-FILE and LOG terminology — came from a deliberate reference to LCARS, the fictional UI from Star Trek: The Next Generation.

This wasn't nostalgia for its own sake. LCARS works as a reference because it encodes a particular attitude: the information is mission-critical, the interface is a tool, and the person using it is competent. It doesn't coddle. It organizes.

The version here isn't faithful to the source material — I've stripped it back considerably, using just the shapes and some of the vocabulary. But the feeling carries. The portfolio reads as a system someone built for a purpose, not a theme someone downloaded.

## Glass as material

A client put it well when we were working through a brand refresh together — the feeling of a material matters as much as its function.

I spent a long time getting the glass panels right. Not the blur effect itself — that's a single CSS property — but the layering of elements on top of it.

The issue is that frosted glass flattens its background into a smear. You get the impression of depth without the reality of it. What I kept working toward was a surface that felt like it had been placed rather than rendered — like you could reach around it and touch the mountain behind it.

What helped: very slight inner borders, subtle gradients suggesting a light source, and careful opacity tuning on the glass tint. None of these elements are visible in isolation. Together they create the sense that the material is real.

## Navigation as architecture

One thing I didn't anticipate: treating navigation as architecture rather than affordance changed how I thought about the whole layout.

A nav bar says "here are your options." An index says "here is how this system is organized." The left rail in this design is an index. It has a hierarchy, it has categories, it has codes. You don't click through it because you're lost — you click through it because you're exploring.

This changed the information architecture downstream. If the nav is an index, the panels are rooms. If the panels are rooms, every panel needs to feel like it's worth being in. That raised the standard for every content view, which made the design better overall.

## What I'd do differently

The modal system went through several iterations and still feels slightly over-engineered in places. The stack-based navigation — where opening a skill from inside a log pushes a new modal rather than navigating — is the right conceptual model, but the animation timing needs more work to feel seamless.

I'd also revisit the light mode. The dark mode was designed first and the light mode was adapted from it. That's the wrong order. The light mode ends up feeling like a translation of something that was designed in another language, and it shows in places.

Both of those are solvable. The bones of the system are right. Sometimes you have to build the thing to find out which parts of it are load-bearing.

That instinct — building to find the load-bearing parts — came up in a coaching conversation I had with a creative director recently. Her framing: you can't pre-visualise a material until you've touched it.
