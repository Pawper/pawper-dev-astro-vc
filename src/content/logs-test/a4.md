I spent four years in a middle school classroom before I became a software engineer. People sometimes ask if the transition was hard. The honest answer is that most of the hard parts had nothing to do with learning to code — they had to do with learning how to think about an audience I couldn't see.

## What the classroom teaches you

Teaching forces a specific kind of rigor. You can't hand a twelve-year-old a dense paragraph and walk away. You have to anticipate the misread, the shortcut, the wrong turn they'll take three steps before they take it. You build the scaffold before you need it.

When I started writing code professionally, I noticed I was doing the same thing. Before naming a function, I'd ask myself: **what will the next person assume this does?** Before structuring a component, I'd trace through how a reader — someone unfamiliar with the context — would parse the hierarchy. This wasn't something I consciously carried over. It just arrived.

## The error message is part of the lesson

One thing teachers learn fast: the moment a student gets it wrong tells you more than the moment they get it right. The wrong answer reveals the underlying model they're working from. Fix the model, and the answers fix themselves.

I apply this to error handling in a way that used to frustrate my colleagues. I spend more time than seems reasonable on error messages. Not just `throw new Error('something went wrong')` — I mean messages that explain *what* went wrong, *where*, and *what to try next*. The error message is the lesson. The crash is the wrong answer. What's the model the user was working from when they triggered it?

## Explaining as a debugging tool

There's a technique teachers use called "explain it back to me." A student who can re-explain a concept in their own words has actually internalized it. A student who can only repeat the original phrasing hasn't.

I use this when I'm stuck on a problem. I open a new file, or sometimes just a comment block, and I start explaining the bug to an imaginary colleague. I describe what I expected, what happened instead, and why I'm confused. About forty percent of the time, I debug the problem in the process of explaining it.

This isn't rubber-duck debugging exactly — it's more deliberate than that. The point is to force myself to find language for the thing I don't understand yet. If I can't explain it, I don't understand it.

## What I brought back

The skills travel in both directions. Teaching made me a better engineer. Engineering made me a better explainer. The two years I spent as a TA in graduate school — after switching fields — were strange and clarifying. I could see the students who were engineering-brained struggling with the same things I'd struggled with: **the audience is always real, even when you can't see them**.

Code has an audience. It's the next engineer, or the user reading the error, or yourself at 11pm six months from now. Write for them. Write like you're going to have to explain it to a room of people who didn't grow up with your assumptions. You might not get it right every time, but the habit of trying changes the work.

The debugging-by-explaining technique has overlap with how I think about documentation — writing for an audience that isn't present is the same skill whether you're teaching or coding.
