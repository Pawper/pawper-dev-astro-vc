A changelog is easy to treat as a formality. You ship something, you write a line about it, you move on. But over time I've started to think of the changelog as a design document written in reverse — a record of the decisions that survived contact with reality.

When I read back through entries from two or three months ago, I can usually tell which changes came from a clear plan and which came from firefighting. The planned ones say things like "added support for X because Y." The firefighting ones say "fixed issue with Z" and stop there. The missing context is the tell.

Writing a changelog entry well forces you to answer: what was the problem, what did we do, and why is this better? If you can't answer all three in a sentence, the change isn't finished — you just stopped working on it.
