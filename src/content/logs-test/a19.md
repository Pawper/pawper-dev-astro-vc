Scope creep is usually treated as a planning failure. The scope grew because the requirements weren't clear, or the estimates were off, or the stakeholders kept adding things. Fix the process and you fix the problem.

That's partly true. But I've seen scope creep happen on well-run projects with clear requirements, and I've noticed it tends to happen most when trust is low. When stakeholders don't trust that future scope will be handled, they try to get everything into the current scope. When engineers don't trust the product direction, they gold-plate the current feature because they don't know if they'll get another chance.

Scope creep is often a signal that someone doesn't believe there's a next time. The fix isn't always a better process — sometimes it's demonstrating that the next iteration is real.
