The first time I pushed a half-finished feature to production I felt a particular kind of dread. Not the fear of breaking something — I had monitoring for that — but the softer anxiety of being seen mid-thought. The interface wasn't polished. The copy wasn't right. A section was placeholder text that I'd been meaning to replace for two weeks.

Nobody noticed. Or rather, the people who noticed weren't bothered by it. What they cared about was that something new was there.

## The case against waiting

There's a particular kind of perfectionism that disguises itself as quality control. You tell yourself you're not shipping because the thing isn't ready. What you mean is: the thing isn't as good as the thing you imagined, and shipping it will prove that.

This is a trap. The imagined version lives in a space where there's no feedback, no tradeoffs, and no users. The real version has to survive contact with all three. The sooner you ship, the sooner the imagined version and the real version can start converging.

## What "public" actually means

Shipping in public doesn't mean announcing every commit. It means building in a way that allows the work to be seen — by users, by collaborators, by your future self — without requiring it to be finished first.

The distinction matters. You're not asking people to evaluate incomplete work. You're building in a way that doesn't rely on secrecy to function.

## The first rough edge

On this project, the first rough edge was the search. It worked, but it was slow, and the results weren't ranked well. I knew it before I shipped it. I shipped it anyway, because a working slow search is more useful than a nonexistent fast one.

Two weeks later, after watching how people actually used it, I knew exactly which parts of the ranking needed fixing. I couldn't have known that from inside the build.
