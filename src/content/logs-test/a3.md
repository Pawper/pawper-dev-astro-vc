I added sound to this portfolio. Subtle clicks and soft tones that fire when you navigate, open panels, hover over interactive elements. The decision felt risky — sound in UI is easy to get wrong and hard to get right — but I've come to think it was one of the better choices in the whole project.

This is an account of why, and how.

## The case for sound in interfaces

Sound is the only sensory channel that's always on. You can look away from a screen, but you can't stop hearing it. This makes sound both powerful and dangerous in UI — it can reinforce feedback, or it can become noise that people immediately mute and resent.

The interfaces that use sound well — old-school terminal beeps, the click of a mechanical keyboard, the thunk of a car door — share a property: the sound is a byproduct of action, not an announcement of it. You're not hearing a sound effect; you're hearing the action itself.

That's the target. The Codex sounds shouldn't say "you clicked a button." They should make clicking the button feel more real.

## What makes a good UI sound

I spent more time than I expected researching this. A few things that are consistently true:

**Short wins.** Sounds longer than 80–120ms start to feel like interruptions. The brain processes short transients as tactile feedback; longer sounds become their own events.

**Sine waves alone feel flat.** A click that sounds natural has some upper harmonics, a little noise, a fast attack and a slightly slower decay. Pure tones feel electronic and out of place.

**Volume matters more than tone.** The wrong volume is the thing most likely to make someone reach for the mute button. Too loud, and even a pleasant sound becomes intrusive. Too quiet, and it doesn't register.

**Consistency is everything.** If some interactions have sound and similar ones don't, the inconsistency reads as a bug. Either commit to a sound vocabulary or use none at all.

## The implementation

All audio in the Codex is generated via the Web Audio API — no audio files, no preloading, no format compatibility issues. Everything is synthesized at the moment it's needed.

The core pattern: create an `AudioContext` lazily (browsers require a user gesture before audio can play), synthesize a short tone with an `OscillatorNode` and a `GainNode`, shape the envelope with `exponentialRampToValueAtTime`, and disconnect when done. The whole thing runs in under 5 milliseconds and allocates minimal memory because the nodes are created and discarded per-sound.

Three sounds cover almost everything:

**Click** — a very short (60ms) descending tone with a quick attack and exponential decay. Used for navigation and explicit button presses.

**Hover** — an even shorter (40ms) soft tone, quieter than the click, with a faster decay. Used for interactive elements on mouse-enter.

**Open** — a slightly longer (120ms) tone that starts lower and rises slightly, suggesting something expanding. Used for modal opens and section reveals.

## React context for audio state

The main challenge was giving every component access to the sound functions without prop-drilling an `onSound` callback through the entire tree.

The solution: a `SoundContext` that wraps the whole app. The context holds a single `AudioContext` instance (created once, on first user interaction) and exports the three named functions: `soundClick`, `soundHover`, `soundOpen`. Any component that needs sound imports from the context and calls the function directly.

This keeps the audio logic entirely separate from component logic. Components don't know how the sounds are generated — they just call a named function and something happens. If I wanted to swap the synthesis approach, replace sounds with audio files, or add a silent mode, none of the consuming components would change.

## The opt-out problem

Sound is a preference, not a feature everyone wants. The Codex exposes a toggle in the header that mutes all sound output. This is wired into the `SoundContext` — the three functions check a `muted` ref before doing anything. The ref (rather than state) is intentional: checking audio state doesn't need to re-render components.

I also respect `prefers-reduced-motion` at the context level. If the user has requested reduced motion, the audio context is initialized in a muted state. This is a slight semantic mismatch — reduced motion is about animation, not sound — but in practice, people who want a quieter interface tend to have that preference set, and it's better to err on the side of less.

## What surprised me

The thing I didn't expect: sound made the interface feel more responsive, even on actions where the visual feedback was already fast.

I think the reason is latency perception. A click that produces instant sound feels faster than a click that produces only visual feedback, even if the visual feedback happens at the same time. The auditory system processes inputs faster than the visual system, and the brain uses the earlier signal to timestamp the event.

The practical upside: I stopped worrying about animation duration as much. If a panel takes 300ms to open, but the open sound fires in 5ms, the action feels immediate. The animation is just the visual confirmation of something that already happened.

That's a good trade.
